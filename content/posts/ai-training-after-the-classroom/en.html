I took part in corporate AX training as an assistant instructor, helping with hands-on exercises and answering questions. AX refers to the process of bringing AI into the workplace and changing how people work. While supporting the training, I kept wondering what would happen after the participants went back to work. Would they be able to use what they had learned here in their own jobs?

While helping with an exercise, I heard someone say, “I think I’ll forget all of this later.” During training, participants are given a sequence to follow and something to create. But once they return to work, they have to decide for themselves what to use AI for in the first place. Hearing that made me think about the steps that still lie between completing an exercise and putting it to practical use.

Starting by Describing Your Own Work

The participants were working professionals with experience in their respective fields. I wished we could have spent more time connecting their knowledge of their work with AI.

Still, even work you know well can be difficult to put into words. To explain a judgment you normally make without much thought, you have to retrace the conditions you considered and what mattered to you. Asking AI for something is similar. Having a desired result in your head is not the same as being able to describe it concretely.

That is why I felt it would help to support the process of drawing out people’s thoughts alongside providing example prompts. This could mean making sense of their work through questions and answers about what they do, where they get stuck, and what they want to change. Rather than writing a fully formed request from the outset, they could make it more specific through a back-and-forth conversation.

People using AI do not need to know all the answers in advance. They can ask about what they do not know, compare the answers with their own experience, and decide what to do next. This was the kind of approach I wanted to share more of.

What Good Content Alone Cannot Provide

The training content was good. What I found limiting was how difficult it was to follow individual questions far enough in a group setting. Even when everyone heard the same explanation, their work and their questions differed. While supporting the exercises, it was not easy to respond in depth to those differences.

AI is a tool people can give their own context to and get help from. Yet in a setting where people were learning about AI, there was limited room to explore that individual context. I felt that, alongside time spent learning the same material together, participants also needed time to think through their own work.

I wondered what training within the same department or a small organizational unit might look like. People could discuss recurring procedures or time-consuming tasks in work they all understood, then try applying AI. If someone who knew the work well and someone helping with AI use developed those ideas together, it seemed that finding something to continue after the training would be easier.

Bringing It Back to the Next Task

This experience made my view of the goal of AX training a little more concrete. What participants have created by the end matters, but so does whether they can think of where to try AI in their next task. Even if they forget some of the mechanics, they could carry their learning forward if they can ask again for what they need and give it a try.

If I help with this kind of training again, I would like to start by asking people to describe work they already know well. I want to help them organize that explanation, find something they could entrust to AI, and try it for themselves.

What this experience made me want to do more of was not introduce a long list of new features, but help someone return to AI in their own work.
