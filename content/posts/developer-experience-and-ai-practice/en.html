When I helped with AI training for people in non-development roles, I thought the gap between those explaining the technology and those using it came from development experience. I assumed developers would find it easier to imagine how to use AI.

Later, while participating as an assistant instructor in training for junior developers, I revisited that assumption. Helping with exercises and answering questions showed me that ways of working with AI can be unfamiliar even to people with development experience. I had assumed that the concepts and working methods I used routinely would also be familiar to other developers.

The participants I met that day were different from what I had expected. I felt I should understand this not as a difference in development ability, but as a difference in each person’s experience using AI.

Hands-On Experience, Not Just Explanations

Development knowledge helps you read and review code produced by AI. But deciding which parts of your work to delegate, what context to explain, and where to exercise your own judgment is a separate matter. Understanding code was not the same as being familiar with how to delegate work to AI.

Supporting the training also made me think about how to share the perspectives and know-how I had developed. I wondered whether explaining features and demonstrating how to use them would be enough for other people to apply them to their own work.

I think people need the experience of applying AI to their actual work and finding it helpful. Even delegating a small task reveals where an explanation was insufficient and what needs to be checked in the result. As those experiences accumulate, it should become easier to judge how much to delegate next time and what to do yourself.

Even when explaining how to use AI, I felt the first step should be to find something worth trying in the other person’s current work, rather than simply passing on the approach I was familiar with.

Who Wrote It, and How to Check It

Reflecting on this experience also made me think about the reliability of AI-written code. What came to mind first was the fact that people make mistakes too.

This does not mean we should take AI errors lightly because people make mistakes. It is closer to saying that a checking process is necessary regardless of who wrote the code. Just as we cannot assume something is correct merely because a person wrote it, we need to examine what an AI-generated result actually does.

That is why I want to put the question “How do we check this result?” alongside “Can we trust AI?” We still have a responsibility to judge whether it behaves as requested, whether any conditions have been missed, and whether it is suitable to use. If we help people use AI, I believe we should cover how to check the results as well as how to obtain them.

Procedures to Repeat and Decisions for People to Make

I also thought it was important to distinguish what to delegate in automation. Work with a clearly defined procedure to repeat is different from work that requires deciding what to do.

The example I thought of was a meeting. Choosing a direction in a meeting requires human judgment. Recording and organizing what was discussed afterward, on the other hand, could be designed as a repeatable procedure. We can distinguish between delegating the decision itself and helping ensure that the work needed after a decision is not overlooked.

If we apply methods for repeating actions without making this distinction, we may lose sight of why we are repeating them. I am interested in an approach where people take responsibility for the purpose of the work and the judgments involved, then identify which of the subsequent procedures AI can help with.

The question that stayed with me after serving as an assistant instructor was how to connect the approaches I had learned with other people’s work. I do not think my way is the right answer for everyone. People do different work and need different kinds of help.

Still, I felt that applying AI at work calls for someone who can help make that connection: someone who listens to the work people do, helps them identify parts they could delegate, and works out how to check the results. Alongside explaining how to use AI well, I became interested in helping other people find ways to use it in their own work.
